How this works

Read step-by-step instructions
  1. Decide whether to set a specific career goal. The initial example is becoming a family medicine physician. You can instead choose “I want my child to try their best.”
  2. Choose family starting conditions.
  3. At each of the five developmental stages, choose the available approach. Carpenter appears only when a specific career goal is selected.
  4. Review the changing career possibilities and the visual growth path, or read the equivalent stage-by-stage text.
  5. Compare satisfaction with the selected parenting goal and the model's occupational-fit proxy. Save the completed run, switch the goal definition, and play again. Review the side-by-side comparison. Different goal definitions use different satisfaction calculations.

There is no single correct sequence. Try a second round with different decisions and compare the results.

    About the model, its inspiration, and its limitations

    This activity was inspired by Dr. Alison Gopnik's Carpenter and Gardener framework (Gopnik, 2017). It places the supplied occupations in an illustrative three-dimensional space. Pay is scaled logarithmically, education is based on an ordered credential scale, and emotional labor comes from the supplied occupation data.

    This page runs on the same model as the main Carpenter or Gardener activity. The child's starting point is drawn from the selected family conditions, with some luck, so two children from the same family start in different places. Each Carpenter stage steps toward the chosen career, with some drift. Each Gardener stage steps further, in whatever direction the child has been growing. After five stages, the child lands on the occupation the model matches them to, which weighs distance, how common the job is, and any missing credential. The model's settings were tuned so that the spread of outcomes resembles real American pay and schooling. It is still a teaching model: the likelihoods it quotes are the model's, not real-world chances of reaching an occupation.

    When a career goal is chosen, goal satisfaction is the share of the distance to that occupation the child covered from where they started, less 8% for each Carpenter stage. When the parent selects “try their best,” there is no career to miss, so satisfaction runs from 50 to 100 and reflects how squarely the life landed on a real occupation compared with other undirected lives. Occupational fit is reported in both cases and falls as the child's final position moves away from the nearest occupation. These scores illustrate the impact of changing the definition of success. They do not measure actual child interests, happiness, parent satisfaction, occupational probabilities, or causal parenting effects. No model result validates one parenting approach as universally better.

    Reference

    Gopnik, A. (2017). The gardener and the carpenter: What the new science of child development tells us about the relationship between parents and children. Picador.

    Open reflection questions
    1. Play with a specific career goal, then select “try their best” and replay. What changes in the definition of goal satisfaction?
    2. Why might a child reach an occupation that is a strong modeled fit without reaching the parent's predetermined career goal?
    3. Which information would be required to measure an individual's actual satisfaction and interests?
    4. How can the growth images and the equivalent text support different ways of understanding the five stages?
